Research notes

Much of this work happened between the first working solver and the current manuscript. These notes follow a few of those steps: building solvers, writing an FFT library, working with crystal defects, and deciding which blocks need an update.

Building the solvers

I began with a three-dimensional phase-field solver for dendritic growth. Getting a reasonable shape was only the first check: I varied anisotropy and seed initialization, inspected the coupled phase and temperature fields, and reorganized the finite-difference loops and memory layout before building parallel versions.

I then built phase-field crystal (PFC), structural PFC (XPFC), and amplitude PFC (APFC) solvers, which represent crystals through density fields or their amplitudes. In APFC, I worked with three complex amplitudes and reconstructed the density field. Abrupt seed boundaries and inconsistent phases could create misleading patterns, so I compared amplitude, phase, and phase-gradient views and tested smoother initial conditions.

Writing an FFT library

I wrote a small FFT library while building the early PFC solver. Starting from Cooley–Tukey decomposition, I worked through recursive and iterative implementations, bit-reversal indexing, and precomputed factors, then compared the implementation with FFTW. The reports also record questions about memory contiguity, temporary buffers, and transform planning.

These experiments made the FFT less of a black box. Later, when moving to cuFFT and MPI, I could separate local transforms from packing, redistribution, and boundary-state exchange. That distinction matters when measuring speed: reducing one update stage does not automatically reduce the full solver’s transform and communication costs.

Grain boundaries & defects

I also used simpler PFC setups, including a small-angle circular grain boundary, to look more closely at defects and boundary motion. A grain inside a surrounding crystal is easier to inspect than a whole polycrystal. I use these exploratory cases to check the solver and the analysis together.

To analyze larger fields, I extract density peaks, calculate local orientations, and use periodic neighbors to group grains. OVITO helps me inspect the particle data; the two-dimensional segmentation and statistics come from my own code. Choices such as neighbor distance, 60° orientation periodicity, and low-density masks can change the statistics, so I check those choices alongside the images.

From thresholds to a framework

The first spectral-activation experiments did not simply work after choosing a threshold. FFT normalization could make almost every block appear active. A fixed change threshold could also behave very differently early and late in an evolution. I compared candidate activity measures, neighboring-block activation, and transition smoothing while watching the real field, spectrum, and activation history together.

This work developed into sp-DBA’s shared framework: state preparation, blockwise model updates, and consistency control, matched to GPU execution. The final design retains the solver’s representation and transform workflow; its acceleration is measured separately for the update stage and the whole run.

One practical lesson was to check more than whether the image looked right. Depending on the model, I tracked field errors, mean density, free energy, and grain statistics, and separated changes in the solution from changes caused by the analysis itself.
