Research

My research asks how to make large-scale physical simulations more efficient without changing the underlying models. I develop solvers, adaptive algorithms, and parallel implementations, then turn simulated fields into grain statistics. This work led to sp-DBA, a framework for adaptive transform-domain computation in materials, pattern formation, and optics.

DBA · Prior work

Phase-field simulations spend much of their time updating regions that barely change. Adaptive mesh refinement reduces this waste, but changing mesh hierarchies can complicate GPU execution. Ruoyao Zhang and Yang Xia developed DBA around hardware-aligned blocks and shared-memory activation decisions on a fixed grid. Their dendritic-growth tests retained full-update morphology and tip-growth accuracy, with near-ideal scaling to 32 GPUs through overlapping computation and communication.

Ruoyao Zhang and Yang Xia. A dynamic block activation framework for continuum models. Nature Computational Science 5, 345–354 (2025).

sp-DBA

I led the design and implementation of sp-DBA with Prof. Yang Xia, bringing hardware-aware adaptivity to transform-domain computation. DBA can leave quiet spatial regions idle, but each Fourier mode spans the whole domain, so its local activation rules cannot simply carry over. We instead adapt which transformed-field blocks are updated while preserving the numerical representation, making this strategy reusable across physical simulations and signal processing. We designed it as a layer around existing solvers, so the equations and numerical methods remain intact. My work covered method design, GPU implementation, validation across three applications, and manuscript preparation.

  1. Hardware-aligned blocks reduce work and thread divergence.

  2. Adaptive updates complement optimized transforms and distributed FFTs.

  3. Solver compatibility preserves equations, discretization, and transformed representations.

  4. Modular integration reuses solver cores with limited model-specific changes.

We tested sp-DBA in phase-field crystal (PFC) models for materials, reaction–cross-diffusion (RCD) for biological patterns, and digital backpropagation (DBP) for optical communications.

Phase-field crystal (PFC)

Materials science · Polycrystalline coarsening

Phase-field crystal (PFC) models resolve atomic-scale periodic order while following microstructure evolution over diffusive times. Their high-order dynamics suit Fourier pseudo-spectral methods. FFTs move the density field between real space and Fourier space, where each coefficient measures a spatial wave. I implemented the solvers and sp-DBA integration, then compared polycrystalline evolution and grain statistics with full-update (Regular) simulations. Reaction–cross-diffusion (RCD)

Biological pattern formation

Reaction–cross-diffusion models describe biological patterns formed by interacting species and coupled transport. I implemented the multicomponent solver and combined sp-DBA with matrix eigendecomposition to reduce repeated Fourier-space work. For 48 components, the combined optimization achieved a 28.1× update-stage speedup over the direct-matrix Regular baseline.

Digital backpropagation (DBP)

Optical communications

Digital backpropagation numerically reverses optical-fiber propagation to recover signals distorted by dispersion, mode coupling, and nonlinear effects. I implemented the split-step solver and adaptive frequency-domain updates. With controlled Gaussian wave packets, sp-DBA reached an 8.4× overall speedup over full-update Regular.

FFT & Distributed FFTs

I wrote an FFT library and compared it with FFTW to understand high-performance implementation. Cooley–Tukey decomposition, bit-reversal reordering, and memory access showed me how algorithmic choices affect runtime.

For distributed PFC, I combined local cuFFT transforms with MPI redistribution and adapted sp-DBA’s state exchange to slab boundaries. On one to eight A800 GPUs, weak-scaling tests reduced per-GPU update time by about 2–3× over Regular. Global transforms and communication remained the main overall cost. Local state exchange supports activation decisions at slab boundaries; it is separate from the global all-to-all communication required by the distributed FFT.

Grain boundaries & defects

Phase-field crystal (PFC) · From density fields to grain statistics

To measure microstructure in single-layer PFC simulations, I built a pipeline from continuous density fields to grain statistics. C++/CUDA routines extract density peaks and export atomic coordinates, in-plane orientations, and quaternions. My Python algorithm reconstructs grains and computes areas and grain-size distributions (GSDs), while OVITO lets me inspect the particle data. This makes it possible to check defects and grain boundaries before interpreting the resulting statistics.

Structural phase-field crystal (XPFC)

XPFC replaces the classical PFC spatial operator with a two-point correlation function C2. Its Fourier transform C^2 sets preferred lattice spacings and symmetry, making a wider range of crystal structures accessible. I implemented Fourier-space solvers and initialization routines to examine crystal growth and polycrystalline evolution.

Amplitude phase-field crystal (APFC)

APFC describes atomic-scale order through slowly varying complex amplitudes Aj, from which the density field ψ is reconstructed. This avoids resolving every lattice oscillation and supports larger-scale simulations. I implemented the amplitude equations in Fourier space and examined grain contact through reconstructed density. Sharp boundaries and defect cores still require sufficient resolution, where the slowly varying approximation needs care.

PF · Dendritic growth

Phase-field models describe moving solid–liquid interfaces without tracking their geometry explicitly. In Kobayashi’s model, crystal growth couples to heat diffusion, while anisotropy sets the preferred branching directions. I implemented a three-dimensional finite-difference solver with OpenMP, MPI, and CUDA versions, and compared how anisotropy, temperature sensitivity, seed size, and undercooling affect dendritic morphology.
