Personal Statement

Five years ago, I imagined a quiet, stable job as a civil servant; two years ago, a well-paid job at a large company. At Hunan University, I began finding direction through research. My advisor, Prof. Yang Xia, gave me room to explore and took my interests seriously. Before our monthly one-on-one meetings, sometimes lasting two or three hours, I read papers, tested ideas, and prepared presentations. His questions exposed gaps in my reasoning and helped me decide what to investigate next. I gradually became more comfortable thinking independently and found that I enjoyed research itself.

I began with density functional theory (DFT), learning C++ along the way. I found myself drawn to the computations and moved toward phase-field modeling and high-performance computing (HPC) in spring 2025. I studied numerical methods, parallel programming, and GPU architecture. Writing my own FFT and comparing it with FFTW made the connection between mathematics and implementation tangible. Fourier basis functions could describe a complicated field through coefficients, yet in some problems much of the spectral amplitude occupied only a small part of Fourier space. Could that structure help us avoid unnecessary calculations? How could we preserve accuracy while skipping updates? I liked following these questions until an optimization stopped feeling like black magic.

My first research project was a phase-field simulation of dendritic growth. I found it fascinating that a relatively simple model could produce intricate patterns without tracking every microscopic detail. Later, phase-field crystal (PFC) simulations drew me toward making the computations more efficient. That led to sp-DBA, which I developed with Prof. Xia for existing transform-domain solvers. We used the evolving spectral structure to choose which blocks needed updates and which could be held. We mapped these decisions onto GPU thread blocks, using smoothed transitions and periodic refreshes to control error. We tested the approach in materials simulation, reaction–cross-diffusion, and optical signal processing. In one multicomponent test, sp-DBA combined with eigendecomposition accelerated Fourier-space updates by up to 28 times. I also ran PFC simulations on a 16,384 × 16,384 grid for ten million steps on a single GPU. I enjoyed taking an idea from mathematics, making it work in code, and finding uses for it across different problems.

I now hope to pursue a PhD in scientific computing and HPC. I want a stronger foundation in numerical analysis, computational physics, parallel algorithms, and computer architecture, and I want to develop methods other researchers can use. I am curious about bringing hardware-aware ideas to different numerical approaches, even when that means learning a new field from scratch. A scientist studying grain boundaries might use a faster solver to follow their evolution over longer times and notice behavior earlier calculations could not reach. I might never work on that question myself. Knowing that something I built helped someone else investigate it would make me very happy.
