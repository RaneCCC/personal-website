Personal Statement

Five years ago, I pictured myself becoming a civil servant and settling into a stable life. Two years ago, I thought I would join a large company and earn a good salary. I had not tried enough things to know what I actually wanted. Graduate school at Hunan University changed that. My advisor, Prof. Yang Xia, introduced me to a world of research that made me curious and gave me the confidence to explore it.

I began with density functional theory and learned C++ along the way. The computing side drew me in more than the electronic-structure problems themselves. In spring 2025, I moved towards phase-field modelling and high-performance computing. I spent much of my time learning the physics, numerical methods and parallel computing that I needed. Ideas that had once seemed distant and abstract became much more interesting when I could write something, run it and see what it did.

A phase-field model of dendritic growth was an early turning point. I liked how a relatively simple description could produce such intricate shapes. It reminded me of taking off a pair of glasses. Some details disappear, but the features you want to understand can still be clear. Learning what a model keeps, what it leaves out and when those choices matter became part of the appeal of computational materials research for me.

As I worked on these simulations, I wanted to understand why a calculation was slow and what a proposed improvement really changed. I want to understand an optimization well enough that it stops feeling like black magic. I enjoy tracing an idea back to its numerical and physical assumptions. Often, something that initially feels obscure becomes quite elegant once I understand the reasoning behind it. That process makes me want to keep learning.

Developing sp-DBA with Prof. Xia brought these interests together. I led the work of turning an idea about selectively updating frequency-space blocks into a framework for existing solvers and parallel hardware. It required me to think about equations, numerical updates and GPU execution together. I found that building the method was as interesting as the research questions it could help answer. I would like to continue this kind of work during a PhD.

I want to strengthen my foundations in numerical analysis, statistical physics and computer architecture. I would also like to explore how ideas from high-performance computing can support other numerical methods, including finite elements, finite volumes and spectral elements. I hope to learn these methods well enough to see where a new computing strategy could make a useful difference. New hardware also interests me, but my main question is what it can make possible for scientific simulations and how to build methods that use it well.

If research is a beach with gold hidden in the sand, I would like to be someone who makes shovels. I like the thought that a method I build could help other people investigate questions that matter to them, including questions I have never considered. Working between physics, mathematics and computing would give me room to keep exploring, while making it a little easier for others to do the same.
