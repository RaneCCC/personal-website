Personal Statement

Five years ago, I would have told you I wanted a quiet, stable job as a civil servant. Two years ago, it would have been a well-paid job at a large company. Looking back, those answers had more to do with what I imagined than with anything I had tried. I spent much of college without a clear direction. That began to change at Hunan University, where my advisor, Prof. Yang Xia, gave me room to explore and took my interests seriously. Before our monthly one-on-one meetings, sometimes lasting two or three hours, I would read papers, test ideas, and prepare presentations carefully. His questions exposed gaps in my reasoning but also helped me decide what to investigate next. Gradually, I became more comfortable thinking independently and discovered that I enjoyed research itself.

I began with density functional theory (DFT), learning C++ along the way, but found myself more interested in the computations than in electronic-structure problems. In spring 2025, I moved toward phase-field modeling and high-performance computing (HPC). I studied numerical methods, parallel programming, and GPU architecture. Writing my own FFT and comparing it with FFTW made the connection between mathematics and implementation tangible. I was struck by how Fourier basis functions describe a complicated field through coefficients and how, in some problems, much of the spectral amplitude occupies only a small part of Fourier space. Could that structure help us avoid unnecessary calculations? How could we reserve accuracy if some updates were skipped? I liked following questions like these until an optimization stopped feeling like black magic.

My first research project was a phase-field simulation of dendritic growth. I found it fascinating that a relatively simple model could produce intricate patterns without tracking every microscopic detail. Later, in phase-field crystal simulations, I became interested in making the computations themselves more efficient. That led to sp-DBA, which I developed with Prof. Xia to introduce adaptive execution into existing transform-domain solvers. We used the evolving spectral structure to decide which blocks needed full updates, which could be skipped, and where smoothed transitions and periodic refreshes were needed to control error. We also mapped these decisions onto GPU thread blocks. We tested the approach in materials simulation, reaction–cross-diffusion, and optical signal processing. In one multicomponent test, sp-DBA combined with eigendecomposition accelerated Fourier-space updates by up to 28 times. I also ran PFC simulations on a 16,384 × 16,384 grid for ten million steps on a single GPU. What I enjoyed most was taking an idea from mathematics, making it work in code, and finding that it could be useful in very different problems.

I now hope to pursue a PhD in scientific computing and HPC. I want a stronger foundation in numerical analysis, computational physics, parallel algorithms, and computer architecture, and I want to use that knowledge to develop methods other researchers can use. I am curious about bringing hardware-aware ideas to different numerical approaches, even when that means learning a new field from scratch. For example, a scientist studying grain boundaries might use a faster solver to follow their evolution over much longer times and notice behavior that earlier calculations could not reach. I might never work on that particular question myself. Knowing that something I built helped someone else investigate it would make me very happy.
