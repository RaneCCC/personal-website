Personal Statement

Five years ago, I would have told you I wanted a quiet, stable job as a civil servant. Two years ago, it would have been a well-paid job at a large company. Looking back, those answers had more to do with what I imagined than with anything I had tried. I spent much of college without a clear direction. That began to change at Hunan University, where my advisor, Prof. Yang Xia, gave me room to explore. Our monthly one-on-one meetings sometimes lasted two or three hours. On one early project, I spent about two months exploring an idea before he helped me change direction. He explained that the exploration had broadened my view, but that the project needed a different focus. I began learning how to judge which questions to pursue, as well as how to answer them.

I began by exploring the electronic properties of materials and learning C++, but found myself more interested in how the calculations worked. In spring 2025, I began pursuing my own interests in phase-field modeling and high-performance computing (HPC), learning to write simulations and run them efficiently on GPUs. I wrote my own Fourier-transform code and compared it with an established library, working through the connection between the mathematics and its implementation. Could we reduce the work a simulation does without losing accuracy? I liked following questions like this until an optimization stopped feeling like black magic.

My first phase-field project simulated the growth of branching crystals. I found it fascinating that a relatively simple model could produce such intricate patterns. Later, some of my crystal simulations kept producing invalid numbers. Working through those failures, I began checking the numerical method as carefully as the code. I carried these questions about reliability and efficiency into sp-DBA, which I developed with Prof. Xia to help existing simulation programs avoid unnecessary work while keeping errors under control. We tested it in materials simulation, pattern formation, and optical signal processing. I also ran large-scale crystal simulations for millions of time steps on a single GPU. What I enjoyed most was taking an idea from mathematics, making it work in code, and finding that it could be useful in very different problems.

I now hope to pursue a PhD in scientific computing and HPC. I want to understand numerical methods and parallel computing more deeply, so I can build tools that other researchers can use. I am curious about how to design numerical methods that work well with modern hardware, even when that means learning a new field from scratch. For example, a scientist studying how boundaries between crystals move might use a faster simulation to follow those changes over much longer times. I might never work on that particular question myself. Knowing that something I built helped someone else investigate it would make me very happy.
